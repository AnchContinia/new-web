The month-end close, re-engineered: a step-by-step playbook

Most closes are slow for the same handful of reasons, and none of them are the accountants. A practical sequence for cutting days without adding headcount.

Continia team27 May 20268 min read

Closes get slow in predictable places. The sequence below fixes them in the order that compounds — each step makes the next one cheaper.

None of it requires more people. Most of it requires deciding, once, what should never reach a human again.

Step 1 — Move the cut-off upstream

Most closes begin with a scramble because documents arrive during the close rather than before it. Set a capture cut-off that is earlier than your posting cut-off, and make the gap visible. What arrives late should be visibly late, not quietly absorbed.

Step 2 — Separate the two kinds of work

There is work that needs an accountant and work that needs a keystroke. Mixing them means the first waits behind the second. Split the queue: untouched invoices post on their own path, exceptions get a named owner and an age.

The one metric worth adding

Untouched rate — the share of documents that go from arrival to posted with no human edit. It predicts close length better than volume, headcount or entity count.

Step 3 — Give approvals a deadline, not a nudge

Approval chasing is the most expensive unbilled activity in finance. Route automatically, set escalation, and let approvers act from wherever they are. The point is not speed for its own sake — it is that the close stops depending on anyone's inbox habits.

Step 4 — Reconcile continuously, not at the end

Bank reconciliation left to the last two days turns every discrepancy into a crisis. Pulled forward and run continuously, the same discrepancies are small, recent and easy to explain.

Step 5 — Make the archive the first place you look

If retrieving a document during close takes more than a few seconds, the close inherits that cost every time a question is asked. An archive with a real audit trail turns a research task into a lookup.

A close does not get shorter because people work faster. It gets shorter because fewer things need a person at all.

What good looks like

  • Documents captured before the period ends, not during the close.
  • An untouched rate you can quote from memory, trending up.
  • An exception queue with owners and ages — and it is short.
  • Approvals that escalate on their own.
  • Nobody rebuilding a reconciliation in a spreadsheet.
